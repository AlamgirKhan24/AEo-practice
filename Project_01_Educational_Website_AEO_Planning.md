## Executive recommendation

Build a trusted, free learning hub that teaches secondary-school students how to study effectively. Organize it around specific learner tasks and questions, publish concise answers supported by useful examples, and connect those answers to deeper lessons and practice tools. Treat answer-engine visibility as an outcome to measure, not a result that markup or wording can guarantee.

This task defines the strategy and plan only. It does not conduct keyword research, create site content, select a platform, or implement technical changes.

## 1. Project overview

The project is to plan and then build an educational website whose information is useful to learners and straightforward for search engines and AI-powered answer experiences to discover, interpret, and cite. AEO (Answer Engine Optimization) here means improving content quality, structure, accessibility, and technical discoverability so a page can serve as a dependable answer source. It complements SEO and good teaching; it is not a separate markup trick or a promise of placement.

| Planning element | Recommendation |
| --- | --- |
| Purpose | Help students learn and apply practical study skills through reliable, readable lessons and reusable tools. |
| Initial niche | Evidence-informed study methods and exam preparation for secondary-school learners, roughly ages 13–18. |
| Primary success | Learners can find, understand, and use an answer. Search visibility and citations are tracked as secondary outcomes. |
| Scope boundary | English-first, geography-neutral guidance at launch. Add curriculum-specific material only after selecting a country, curriculum, and qualified reviewers. |
| Editorial standard | Every lesson has a named author or reviewer, source trail, review date, stated audience/level, and correction route. |

### Purpose and problems addressed

Students often encounter advice that is generic, contradictory, or disconnected from the next action they need to take. The site will turn common learning challenges into short explanations, worked examples, and usable routines. It will also give parents and educators a clear resource they can share without needing to translate specialist terminology.

- A learner does not know how to start revising or choose a method for a subject.
- Study advice says what to do but does not show how to do it, how long to try it, or how to check whether it worked.
- Useful answers are spread across long videos, social posts, and pages with weak navigation or unclear sourcing.
- Families and teachers need age-appropriate explanations and practical resources they can adapt.
- Search and AI systems may struggle to identify the exact topic, scope, author, and evidence behind a page if the site is vague or poorly structured.
## 2. Website concept

Working concept: “StudyReady” — a free, practical study-skills library for secondary-school students. Each resource addresses one learning task, such as planning a revision week, using flashcards well, or approaching an unfamiliar exam question. It pairs a direct answer with explanation, a concrete example, common mistakes, and an optional printable or practice activity.

The site should distinguish learning guidance from curriculum or clinical advice. Where recommendations depend on an exam board, local standards, disability accommodation, or a learner’s wellbeing, state the boundary and point to an appropriate qualified source.

### Positioning and editorial promise

“Clear study guidance students can try today, with the reasoning and sources visible.” This is a proposed positioning statement, not a claim of proven outcomes. Avoid promises such as guaranteed grades, fixed learning styles, or one method that works for everyone.

## 3. Target audience

| Audience | Need and context | Content response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Primary: students ages 13–18 | Needs a clear next step, manageable language, examples, and mobile-friendly access. May be studying independently or under time pressure. | Short answer first; sequenced steps; worked examples; check-your-understanding prompts; printable tools. |
| Secondary: parents and caregivers | Wants practical ways to support routines without taking over the learning. | Shareable guides, conversation prompts, and clear notes on what is age- or context-dependent. |
| Secondary: teachers and tutors | Needs credible material that can be assigned or used in a lesson. | Stable topic pages, transparent sources, classroom-friendly downloads, and clear content levels. |
| Site contributors and reviewers | Needs repeatable standards for accuracy, attribution, accessibility, and updates. | Editorial templates, reviewer bylines, source logs, version/review dates, and correction workflow. |

Audience and geography must be validated in the research phase. For the initial content inventory, use broad school-stage language and do not imply alignment with any national curriculum.

## 4. Website architecture

Use a small set of stable learning hubs. Each article should sit in one primary category, link to prerequisite or next-step resources, and use descriptive page titles and headings. Navigation labels should reflect learner language rather than internal SEO terminology.

| Page / section | Purpose and core components |
| --- | --- |
| Home | Explain who the site serves; route learners to popular tasks, topic hubs, and a simple “start here” path. |
| Study Skills hub | Index the core skill areas and explain how they fit together; avoid a thin tag archive. |
| Revision and Retrieval Practice | Teach recall, spaced review, flashcards, and self-testing with examples and limits. |
| Planning and Focus | Provide routines for planning a study session, managing workload, and reducing distractions without medical claims. |
| Notes and Understanding | Show ways to process lessons, summarize ideas, and check comprehension, with sample notes. |
| Exam Preparation | Explain how to interpret prompts, practice under realistic conditions, review mistakes, and plan before an exam. |
| Subject Learning Guides | Demonstrate study methods in selected subjects. Start with a small, reviewed set after demand and expertise are confirmed. |
| Tools and Downloads | Offer practical planners, checklists, and practice templates. Each download has an accessible HTML explanation and a labeled file. |
| About and Editorial Policy | State mission, intended audience, author/reviewer qualifications, sourcing policy, corrections process, and funding or conflicts. |
| Authors and Reviewers | Give useful biographies and relevant expertise; link bylines to these pages. |
| Contact and Corrections | Provide a route to report an error, request an accommodation, or suggest a topic. |
| Search, sitemap, and utility pages | Support navigation and crawling. Keep utility pages out of editorial hubs when they add no learning value. |

### Suggested hierarchy

Home → Study Skills → [skill hub] → [specific lesson] → related lesson or tool. Keep URLs descriptive and durable, for example /study-skills/active-recall/ rather than a date-based or campaign URL. Use breadcrumbs in the visible interface when they help learners understand where they are.

## 5. AEO strategy

### 5.1 Find real question opportunities

Build the question set during the next research task; the examples below are hypotheses to investigate, not measured demand or keyword findings. Group questions by the learner’s job and intent. Prioritize questions that the site can answer accurately and demonstrate with its own examples.

| Question cluster | Example questions to validate | Likely useful page format |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Method choice | What is active recall? How is it different from rereading? When should I use flashcards? | Definition plus comparison, example, and “when to use it” guidance. |
| How-to / routine | How do I plan a revision week? How long should a study session be? | Steps, sample schedule, adaptable template, and cautions. |
| Troubleshooting | Why can’t I remember what I studied? What should I do when I get a practice question wrong? | Causes framed carefully, diagnostic checks, practical response, further reading. |
| Exam task | How do I break down an exam question? How should I review a practice test? | Worked example, annotated thinking, checklist, and common errors. |
| Audience / level | How can a 14-year-old prepare for exams? How can a parent help with revision? | Age-aware answer with boundaries and links to relevant learner/parent resources. |

Research inputs: Search Console queries once available; Google Trends for comparative interest patterns (not absolute volume); autocomplete and related searches; teacher/tutor interviews; student interviews; site search logs; and question research tools such as AlsoAsked or AnswerThePublic. Record the query, audience, intent, evidence of need, existing coverage, confidence, and proposed action. Do not invent volume or difficulty where no reliable source is available.

### 5.2 Use an answer-ready content pattern

1. Title the page around one clear learner question or task. Use a single descriptive H1 and a logical H2/H3 outline.
1. Open with a short, direct response that defines the scope. Follow with the explanation, steps, examples, exceptions, and sources needed to use the answer safely.
1. Make the main answer visible as normal page text. Do not put essential learning material only in an image, downloadable file, animation, or client-only widget.
1. Use specific nouns, consistent terminology, and explicit references instead of “it,” “this method,” or undefined jargon. Define unfamiliar terms at first use.
1. Show original teaching value: worked examples, diagrams with text equivalents, practice questions, sample schedules, or a decision aid.
1. Separate generally applicable guidance from curriculum- or learner-specific guidance. Label uncertainty and link to primary evidence or expert sources.
1. Name the author and reviewer where appropriate; add a concise biography, sources, published date, and a meaningful review date. Update dates only after substantive review.
1. Link from hubs to lessons and between prerequisite, related, and next-step lessons using descriptive link text. Add visible breadcrumbs when useful.
1. Use plain language, descriptive headings, readable contrast, keyboard-accessible controls, captions/transcripts where relevant, and useful alternative text for informative images.
Do not write answers solely for bots or force every article into a shallow FAQ. A concise answer is useful when it helps the reader, but sufficient context and instruction should remain on the page.

### 5.3 Structured data opportunities

Structured data is a standard way to label facts about a page in machine-readable form. Use JSON-LD where practical, keep it consistent with visible content, and validate it after release. Markup can aid interpretation or eligibility for specific search features; it does not guarantee ranking, citation, or display.

| Candidate type | Where it may fit | Implementation note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Organization / EducationalOrganization | About, site identity, and publisher details. | Use the most accurate organization type and stable identity details. Avoid implying accreditation or official status. |
| Person | Author and reviewer biographies. | Connect named people to their actual roles and expertise; do not add unsupported credentials. |
| Article / BlogPosting | Substantive dated lessons and editorial articles. | Keep headline, author, dates, and image aligned with visible page information. |
| BreadcrumbList | Lessons within a genuine site hierarchy. | Represent the learner’s path, and ensure the visible breadcrumb matches the markup. |
| WebSite | Site-level identity and name. | Use only accurate site-level information. |
| LearningResource / Course | A structured lesson, learning resource, or actual course when its properties genuinely describe it. | Schema.org types can describe content; do not assume they create a Google rich result. Add only supported, useful properties and visible facts. |
| FAQPage / HowTo | Not a launch priority for this project. | Google’s display support has been narrowed/removed for many sites and How-to rich results are deprecated. Keep Q&A or steps for learners when useful, but do not sell markup as a visibility tactic. |

Google’s Education Q&A feature has narrow requirements and is intended for qualifying education flashcard questions. Consider it only if the site later publishes eligible, visible flashcards and meets the current guidelines; it is not a general FAQ shortcut.

### 5.4 Clarity, credibility, and accessibility

- Clarity: one primary topic per URL, meaningful page title, concise opening, structured explanation, examples, and a clear next action.
- Credibility: visible authorship and review responsibility, primary-source citations, transparent corrections, no fabricated outcomes, and clear disclosure of sponsorship or AI assistance where readers would reasonably expect it.
- Accessibility: semantic HTML, heading order, keyboard support, color contrast, descriptive links, captions and transcripts, responsive layouts, and accessible downloadable documents. Treat accessibility as a core user requirement, not a search trick.
- Technical access: ensure public lesson pages return successfully, can be crawled, have indexable content, and are included in a maintained XML sitemap. Use robots rules for crawl control and noindex where deliberate exclusion from search is required.
- Restraint: no fabricated “AI-ready” claims, unsupported llms.txt promises, mass-produced near-duplicate question pages, or schema that does not match the visible page.
## 6. Tools and resources

Select tools according to actual access, budget, and CMS. The stack below mixes free first-party diagnostics with optional paid research tools; tool data is evidence to interpret, not a substitute for audience insight.

| Need | Recommended tools | Use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Question and topic research | Google Search Console; Google Trends; autocomplete/related searches; AlsoAsked or AnswerThePublic; student/teacher interviews. | Find actual queries and language, compare topic patterns, and confirm user needs. Search Console requires a verified site and has no prelaunch query data. |
| Content planning and quality | A shared content inventory in Sheets; editorial brief template; Google Docs or Word; optional Semrush/Ahrefs for competitive topic discovery. | Track intent, owner, reviewer, source list, status, canonical URL, and review date. Do not treat optimization scores as quality proof. |
| Structured data validation | Schema.org Validator; Google Rich Results Test; Search Console enhancement reports where applicable. | Check vocabulary syntax, Google-supported feature eligibility, and post-release issues. A clean test is not a display guarantee. |
| Technical SEO | Google Search Console URL Inspection and Page Indexing reports; Bing Webmaster Tools; Screaming Frog SEO Spider or Sitebulb; PageSpeed Insights/Lighthouse. | Inspect crawl/index access, page titles, links, sitemap, rendering, mobile usability, and performance. Start with free first-party reports. |
| AEO performance evaluation | A baseline question set run periodically in selected answer engines; Search Console; Bing Webmaster Tools; GA4 or privacy-appropriate analytics; a citation log. | Track impressions, clicks, landing pages, referral visits, and observed citations/mentions with date, exact prompt, engine, location, and cited URL. Separate observed presence from inferred cause. |

Manual answer-engine checks are snapshots: results vary by query, user context, product, and time. Keep the prompts and conditions consistent, record both citations and errors, and avoid claiming causal impact from a small sample.

## 7. Project roadmap

Indicative sequence for a small team; durations are planning ranges, not commitments. Research and approval gates should determine the size of the pilot. Do not draft a large content library before validating the audience, niche, and review capacity.

| Phase | Work and decision gate | Indicative timing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. Discovery and scope | Confirm audience, geography, curriculum boundary, organizational purpose, subject expertise, constraints, and success definition. Gate: approved project brief. | Week 1 |
| 2. Audience and question research | Interview representative learners and educators; gather search questions and competing page examples; cluster by intent; note evidence and gaps. Gate: prioritized question map with uncertainty recorded. | Weeks 2–3 |
| 3. Information architecture | Validate hub/page taxonomy, navigation labels, internal-link paths, URL conventions, and page templates. Gate: approved sitemap and page inventory. | Week 3 |
| 4. Editorial and technical specification | Set source/reviewer standards, accessibility requirements, metadata patterns, schema eligibility, CMS requirements, analytics baseline, and crawl/index acceptance checks. Gate: signed-off templates and acceptance checklist. | Week 4 |
| 5. Pilot content and design | Create a small, representative group of reviewed pages and tools; test comprehension with learners; refine layout and content model. Gate: subject reviewer and user feedback accepted. | Weeks 5–7 |
| 6. Build and QA | Implement templates, navigation, internal links, sitemap, analytics, and only applicable structured data. Check mobile, keyboard/accessibility, performance, rendering, crawl access, broken links, and index directives. Gate: launch checklist passes. | Weeks 7–9 |
| 7. Launch and submission | Publish approved pages; verify canonical URLs and responses; submit sitemap in webmaster tools; inspect priority URLs; confirm measurement collection. | Week 10 |
| 8. Monitoring and iteration | Review indexing and errors, content feedback, organic query patterns, referrals, and a repeatable answer-engine sample. Update inaccurate or underperforming pages based on evidence. Gate: first review and prioritized backlog. | Weeks 11–14, then ongoing |
| 9. AEO audit | Audit a representative URL sample for answer clarity, evidence, structure, markup fidelity, accessibility, crawlability, citations, and content freshness. Compare to baseline and state limits. | End of pilot and quarterly |

### Measurement framework

Set a baseline before launch. Use leading indicators that the team can influence and outcomes that matter to learners. Do not set numerical targets until a real baseline, site scale, and capacity are known.

| Measure | Example evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Coverage and quality | Share of priority questions mapped to a useful page; expert review completion; source coverage; learner comprehension feedback. |
| Technical eligibility | Important URLs crawlable and indexable; sitemap processed; no unintended noindex; structured data matches visible page; accessibility defects resolved. |
| Search discovery | Indexed priority URLs; impressions, clicks, CTR, query/page patterns from Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools. |
| Answer-engine presence | Observed citation/mention rate across a fixed prompt set, engine list, and schedule; citation accuracy and destination quality. |
| Learner usefulness | Task completion feedback, downloads or tool use, return visits, and corrections/clarification requests, interpreted with privacy in mind. |

## 8. Expected final deliverables

- Approved project brief with audience, niche, geography, editorial boundaries, and measurable objectives.
- Audience and question research file with source notes, intent clusters, priority rationale, and explicit unknowns.
- Validated site map, page inventory, navigation labels, and internal-link model.
- Editorial playbook, page brief template, quality checklist, citation/reviewer standard, and update/correction workflow.
- Technical and accessibility requirements for the chosen CMS, including crawl/index, sitemap, analytics, and structured-data specifications.
- Pilot content set and reusable learning-resource templates, after research and approvals.
- Launch QA record, measurement baseline, monitoring dashboard or scorecard, and first AEO audit with prioritized actions.
