Active recall: how to remember what you study

StudyReady Editorial Team · Draft prepared 22 November 2026 · General secondary-school study support

Active recall means trying to bring an idea to mind before looking at the answer.

Active recall is a way to study by retrieving information from memory. Instead of rereading a page first, close the notes and try to explain, list, draw, or answer questions about what you learned. Then check the result and correct gaps.

Why does active recall help?

Trying to retrieve an idea shows what you can explain and what still needs work. It turns revision into a check of learning rather than familiarity. A wrong or incomplete answer is useful information: reopen a reliable source, correct the gap, and try again later.

How can I try it?

Choose a small topic. Review it for understanding, hide the material, and write or say what you remember. Compare your answer with a reliable source, correct errors, and return to the topic after some time.

What is an example?

After studying a process, close the book and draw its stages from memory. Compare labels and connections with the source, then correct the diagram. Feedback matters because recall without correction can leave misunderstandings in place.

When should I use another approach?

If a topic is new, begin with an explanation or worked example. Then use retrieval to check what you learned. For complex skills, combine recall with practice problems and feedback.

Common questions

Is active recall the same as rereading?

No. Rereading exposes you to the material; active recall asks you to bring an answer to mind before checking.

What if I cannot remember?

Review a smaller section, use a hint, and try again after you understand the material.
